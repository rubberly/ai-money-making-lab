## The seven methods, as presented

Legend: **[Said]** = explicitly stated in the video · **[Interp]** = my interpretation.

### Method 1: AI-enhanced property listing photos + walkthrough videos (Gadzhi) · 00:51–04:56 · **B tier**
- [Said] Real estate agents and property owners post "rushed phone pictures with bad lighting" and lack the time or budget for a photographer (01:02–01:21).
- [Said] Workflow: browse listings on Airbnb, Zillow, Property24 or Booking.com (01:36–01:45). Feed the photos into an AI image platform ("Higgsfield") with a prompt to improve them (01:57–02:09). Then generate a "cinematic walkthrough video" from those photos (02:15–02:34).
- [Said] Why clients pay: more inquiries, and time saved, because the video "does the first viewing for them" (02:45–03:09).
- [Said] Lead generation: names, emails and phone numbers "already sat here on online property listings." Pick listings with bad photos, send before/afters as a free sample, and keep one walkthrough as a portfolio piece, since walkthroughs are "a little bit more expensive to generate" (03:13–03:43). The walkthrough is the upsell (03:43–03:50).
- [Said] Price: about **$100** for a walkthrough plus improved images. Cost is "a few dollars in credits". Monthly retainers later. "$5,000 to $10,000 a month" at scale (03:56–04:13).
- [Said] Requirements: a laptop and a Higgsfield subscription. "You can land your first client this week" (04:20–04:28).
- [Said] Competition: "low… at least for now" (04:31–04:42). Downside: it's a service, so "your time is the product" (04:44–04:51).
- [Not mentioned] Disclosure rules for AI-altered listing photos, platform bans on misrepresentative images, or who owns the listing photos. See seven_methods.md.

### Method 2: Repurposing creators' long-form content (Tilbury) · 05:00–09:52 · **A tier**
- [Said] Creators, podcasters and business owners publish long-form content and "know they should be turning that into newsletters, short clips, X posts, and LinkedIn posts" (05:11–05:27).
- [Said] Why not just use ChatGPT themselves: without telling the AI "how the creator actually talks… you just get a generic robotic post", and busy people don't have time to set that up (05:28–05:46).
- [Said] Workflow (05:48–07:21): (1) pick a creator with long-form content. (2) Get a transcript from "an AI transcripting platform", unnamed but "pretty cheap". (3) Create a dedicated **Claude Project** per client, upload "everything you can find on them", and write specific project instructions. (4) Paste the transcript. The output is a newsletter, short-form scripts, an X post and a LinkedIn post "all in the client's voice."
- [Said] First client: pick creators active on one platform "but nowhere else", e.g. "a great podcast, but no newsletter". Repurpose a recent episode and send it as a free sample. Before sending, read it through, tweak it and "check that it's accurate… this quality control is what they'll end up paying me" (07:23–07:59).
- [Said] Price: monthly, by volume, "a few hundred to a couple of $1,000 a month per client" (08:05–08:16). Costs: a Claude subscription and a transcription tool (08:22–08:28).
- [Said] Start time: "within a few days" if "you can tell good writing from boring writing" (08:31–08:36). Competition: "a decent amount", but most competitors "just paste a transcript into an AI and send back whatever comes out" (08:37–08:57).
- [Said] At 09:07–09:52 the video promotes the free 5-day "AI Income Challenge" starting **November 7**.

### Method 3: Dead-lead reactivation (Gadzhi) · 09:55–13:44 · **B tier**
- [Said] Businesses (a dentist, gym, roofer, med spa) hold "hundreds, sometimes even thousands" of old leads who never bought (09:57–10:26).
- [Said] Workflow: import the business's old lead list into **HighLevel** (demoed with a "fake list" for a dental clinic). Use Claude to write natural messages. Use HighLevel's **Conversation AI** to handle replies, configured with the business, services, prices and the goal of booking an appointment (10:55–12:00).
- [Said] Pricing: performance-based, "$50 to a couple $100 for each appointment that gets booked and shows up". "Around $10,000 a month" with a handful of clients (12:02–12:58).
- [Said] **Compliance warnings, the only ones in the video**: message only people who previously opted in to that business, never a bought list, check country texting rules, always provide an opt-out (12:16–12:40).
- [Said] Setup: "a week or so" (13:07–13:16). Competition: better known, but "demand is still bigger than the supply" (13:17–13:33).

### Method 4: Website makeovers for local businesses (Tilbury) · 13:46–17:46 · **between B and C tier**
- [Said] Local business sites are slow, dated and break on phones. Owners avoid redesigns because of cost and delay (13:49–14:10).
- [Said] Workflow: search Google Maps (demo: "gardeners in Kent") for businesses with bad websites. Use an AI website builder (name not spoken) with a detailed prompt built from their services, hours and reviews. Check it on mobile. Email it as a free sample ("I noticed your website wasn't working well on mobile…"). If they like it, charge to finish and launch it, then offer monthly upkeep (14:22–16:31).
- [Said] Price: "a few hundred to a couple of $1,000" per site, plus a monthly fee for hosting and updates (16:32–16:48).
- [Said] Start time: "straight away". The skill to learn is what makes a local-business site convert: clear services, easy contact, mobile layout (16:53–17:12).
- [Said] Competition: crowded. Stand out by building sites "that get customers to call or book". Mostly one-off jobs (17:13–17:34).

### Method 5: AI-generated UGC-style ads (Gadzhi) · 17:48–21:21 · **A tier "for potential", with a warning**
- [Said] Brands need constant new ads. UGC ads used to require shipping products to creators (17:52–18:30).
- [Said] Workflow: pick an AI ad tool (demo: Higgsfield). Upload an avatar image and product images (a lip plumper). Script the action and the words. In the demo, the AI avatar says "everyone kept asking me what I was wearing on my lips… I've had so many compliments" (18:41–19:47). Send the result to the brand. Check "the product, the claims, and the branding" before use (19:50–20:04).
- [Said] Pricing: brands pay real UGC creators "$100 to $600 or more for a single video". Retainers of "a few $1,000 a month per client" (20:09–20:26).
- [Said] Caveats: making an ad that *sells* is a different skill (hooks, angles). It's "the most crowded idea" here. Only A tier "if you're willing to learn how ads actually work" (20:27–21:18).
- [Interp/legal flag] The demo script has an AI-generated person describing a personal experience and compliments received. Under the FTC's 2024 rule on fake reviews and testimonials, that is the kind of testimonial from a person who doesn't exist that the rule targets. See seven_methods.md.

### Method 6: AI customer-support chatbots for businesses (Tilbury) · 21:23–24:42 · **B tier**
- [Said] Businesses answer the same questions repeatedly. An untrained chatbot "gives the wrong answers, which is worse than having no chatbot at all". The value is in setting it up properly, including handoff to a human (21:37–22:13).
- [Said] Workflow: pick a tool ("TDO", likely Tidio, or **Chatbase**). Feed it the website, FAQs and returns policy. Set rules ("only answer using the information provided… if you don't know, say so and offer to pass the customer to a human"). Test thoroughly, "the bit most people skip" (22:16–23:52).
- [Said] Pricing: one-off setup fee plus a monthly fee. **No dollar figures were given** (23:53–23:59).
- [Said] Setup takes an afternoon with no coding. Competition is "medium to high". Downsides: businesses can buy the tools themselves, and "if the bot gets something wrong, the blame lands on you" (24:00–24:40).

### Method 7: Not revealed ("S tier") · 24:46–26:24
- [Said] Both hosts say it "beats everything else on this board", but they **do not say what it is**. They direct viewers to register for the free AI Income Challenge (Nov 7, 5 days) to learn it (24:49–26:13).
- **Missing information:** method 7's content, cost, workflow and evidence are all unknown. The $373/day figure in the description is a marketing claim with no evidence in the video. I did not register for the event, since that requires submitting personal data to a third party.
- [Interp] Pattern: the description advertises a figure ("$373/day"), and the free event is the top of a sales funnel (Gadzhi's education business). The challenge almost certainly ends with a paid offer, but this is **unverified**. Treat the event as marketing, not as part of the evidence base.
